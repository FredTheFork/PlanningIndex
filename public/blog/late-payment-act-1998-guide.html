Late payment is the silent killer of UK small businesses. Every year, thousands of sole traders and freelancers face cash flow crises because clients pay late — or don't pay at all. The Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 exists to protect you. But understanding your rights and knowing how to enforce them is critical to getting paid on time.

What is the Late Payment Act 1998?

The Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 is UK legislation that gives you the right to:

  • Charge statutory interest on late commercial payments
  • Claim fixed compensation on each late payment
  • Recover reasonable costs of collecting the debt

The Act was strengthened by EU Directive 2011/7/EU (retained in UK law post-Brexit), which introduced the current compensation amounts and improved rights.

Automatic Rights

These rights apply automatically to business-to-business contracts. You don't need to state them in your contract — but doing so educates clients about consequences and encourages timely payment.

Who Does the Act Apply To?

The Act applies to:

Who Can Claim

  • Sole traders — any self-employed individual
  • Freelancers and contractors — providing services to businesses
  • Small businesses — any business supplying goods or services
  • Large businesses — claiming from other businesses

Who Must Pay

  • Any UK business — large or small
  • Public sector organisations — government bodies, councils, NHS
  • Charities and non-profits — if they're operating as a business

What's Covered

  • Commercial debts — goods or services supplied to another business
  • Contracts for goods and services — not consumer transactions
  • Business-to-business transactions — not B2C

What's Not Covered

  • Consumer transactions — selling to individuals (consumer law applies instead)
  • Agreements where interest is already built in — credit agreements, hire purchase
  • Mortgage payments — different regulations apply

Statutory Interest Rates Explained

The Act provides a statutory interest rate that kicks in once payment is late:

Current Rate

Statutory Interest Rate = Bank of England base rate + 8%

Current Calculation (May 2026)

Bank of England base rate: 5.25% (check current rate at bankofengland.co.uk)
Statutory addition: 8%
Total statutory interest rate: 13.25% per annum

How Interest Is Calculated

Interest is calculated daily on the outstanding debt. Formula:

Daily interest = (Debt amount x Interest rate) ÷ 365

Example: £1,000 debt at 13.25% interest

  • Daily interest: (£1,000 x 13.25%) ÷ 365 = £0.36 per day
  • After 30 days late: £0.36 x 30 = £10.96 interest
  • After 60 days late: £0.36 x 60 = £21.92 interest

Interest Accrues Until Payment

Interest continues to build until the debt is paid in full. For long-overdue debts, this can add significantly to the amount owed.

Fixed Compensation Amounts

On top of interest, you're entitled to fixed compensation for each late payment:

Compensation Amounts

  • Debts under £1,000: £40 compensation
  • Debts £1,000 - £9,999.99: £70 compensation
  • Debts £10,000+: £100 compensation

Compensation Applies Per Invoice

The compensation amount is based on the individual invoice, not the total debt. If a client owes multiple invoices, you can claim compensation on each one.

Example: Three unpaid invoices of £800 each = £40 x 3 = £120 compensation.

Compensation Is Automatic

You don't have to chase the client for months to claim compensation — it applies automatically once payment is late. However, you should state your rights clearly to put the client on notice.

Recovery Costs

Beyond fixed compensation, you can also claim reasonable costs of recovering the debt. This includes:

  • Debt collection agency fees
  • Solicitor fees (before court action)
  • Court fees (if proceeding to legal action)

When Does the Clock Start?

Understanding when payment becomes "late" is crucial for enforcing your rights:

If You Have a Contract with Payment Terms

Payment is late after the agreed payment term expires. For example:

  • Net 30 terms: Payment is late on day 31
  • Net 14 terms: Payment is late on day 15
  • Due on receipt: Payment is late the next day

If You Have No Agreed Payment Terms

Under the Act, if no payment term is agreed, payment is due within:

  • 30 days for public authorities
  • 30 days for businesses (unless otherwise agreed)

Always Specify Payment Terms

Never rely on the default 30 days. State your terms clearly in your contract and on every invoice: "Payment due within 14 days of invoice date." A specific due date removes all ambiguity.

If the Contract Requires Client Acceptance

If your contract says payment is due after client "acceptance" or "approval" of work:

  • The client must not unreasonably delay acceptance
  • If they do, the 30-day default kicks in from when acceptance was reasonably due
  • Avoid "acceptance" clauses in your contract — use fixed payment dates instead

Your Rights Under the Act

The Act gives you several powerful rights:

1. Right to Statutory Interest

You can charge the statutory interest rate from the day payment becomes late until the date of actual payment. This is a right, not a penalty — it's compensation for being deprived of your money.

2. Right to Fixed Compensation

The compensation amounts (£40/£70/£100) are automatic and don't require proof of loss. They're designed to cover your administrative costs of chasing payment.

3. Right to Recovery Costs

Any reasonable costs you incur to recover the debt can be added to the amount claimed.

4. Right to Refuse Further Work

While the Act doesn't explicitly grant this, common law allows you to suspend performance if the other party breaches the contract. Non-payment is a breach, so you can stop work.

5. Right to Retain Title

If you supply goods, a "retention of title" clause in your contract means ownership doesn't pass until payment is received. This protects your goods if the client goes insolvent.

Step-by-Step Enforcement Guide

Here's the complete process for enforcing your late payment rights:

Step 1: Issue a Proper Invoice

  • Include all required elements (see our guide on invoice best practices)
  • State payment terms clearly: "Payment due within 14 days"
  • Include specific due date

Step 2: Send a Payment Reminder

3-5 days before the due date, send a friendly reminder:

"Just a quick reminder that invoice #123 for £X is due on [date]. Please let me know if you have any questions."

Step 3: The Due Date Passes

Payment is now officially late. Your rights under the Act begin.

Step 4: Send an Overdue Notice (Day 1-3 Late)

Firm but professional reminder:

"Invoice #123 is now overdue. Please arrange payment immediately to avoid late payment charges under the Late Payment Act 1998."

Step 5: Send a Formal Demand (Day 7-14 Late)

Invoke your legal rights:

"Invoice #123 is [X] days overdue. Under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998, I am now entitled to statutory interest and compensation. A revised invoice is attached reflecting these charges. Total due: £[amount]. Please pay within 7 days."

Step 6: Send a Letter Before Action (Day 21-28 Late)

Final demand before legal action:

"This is my final attempt to resolve this matter before commencing legal proceedings. You have 14 days to pay £[amount including interest and compensation] or I will issue a claim through the county court without further notice."

Step 7: Issue County Court Claim

If no response, file a claim using Money Claim Online (MCOL) or through the County Court.

Writing a Letter Before Action

A Letter Before Action (LBA) is your last chance to resolve the matter before court. Make it effective:

Essential Elements

  • Your details: Name, address, reference number
  • Their details: Debtor's name and address
  • Debt details: Original amount, invoice number, date, what it was for
  • Current amount: Including interest, compensation, and costs
  • Deadline: 14 days (standard) or 7 days (for undisputed debts)
  • Consequence: "Court proceedings will be started without further notice"
  • Payment details: How they can pay

Template LBA Text

Dear [Client Name],

Re: Outstanding Payment — Invoice #[Number] — FINAL NOTICE

Despite previous reminders, the above invoice remains unpaid. As of [date], the amount outstanding is:

  • Original debt: £[amount]
  • Statutory interest: £[amount] (calculated at 13.25% from [due date] to [current date])
  • Late payment compensation: £[40/70/100]
  • Total now due: £[total]

This is a formal notice under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998.

If payment is not received by [date — 14 days from letter date], I will commence legal proceedings in the County Court to recover the debt plus court fees and further costs. This will result in:

  • A County Court Judgment (CCJ) being registered against you
  • Additional court fees and enforcement costs being added
  • Potential enforcement action including bailiff attendance

To avoid this, please pay £[total] to [bank details] by [date].

Yours sincerely,
[Your name]

Keep Proof of Sending

Send the LBA by email with read receipt, and first class post (get a certificate of posting from the Post Office). Courts accept email as valid notice if you can prove delivery.

When and How to Take Legal Action

If the LBA deadline passes without payment, proceed to legal action:

Small Claims Track

For most sole trader debts, this is the appropriate route:

  • Jurisdiction: Debts up to £10,000 in England and Wales
  • Cost: £25-£455 fee depending on amount claimed
  • Lawyer: Not required (can represent yourself)
  • Time: 4-6 months on average
  • Risk: You can recover most fees from the losing party

Money Claim Online (MCOL)

For debts under £100,000 owed by no more than 2 defendants:

  • Register at moneyclaim.gov.uk
  • Complete online form
  • Pay fee (added to claim)
  • Claim is served to defendant

What Happens Next

  1. Claim issued: Defendant receives claim form
  2. 14 days to respond: They must acknowledge or defend
  3. 28 days total: If no defence, you can request default judgment
  4. If defended: Case goes to allocation, then hearing
  5. Judgment: Court orders payment

If They Still Don't Pay

After judgment, enforcement options include:

  • Warrant of control: Bailiffs seize goods to sell
  • Attachment of earnings: Deducted from salary
  • Charging order: Charge on their property
  • Third party debt order: Freeze their bank account
  • Statutory demand: Precursor to bankruptcy (debts over £750)

Before Suing, Check Solvency

There's no point suing a bankrupt client. If the company is dissolved or the individual has no assets, you won't recover anything. A County Court Judgment is only useful if it can be enforced against real assets.

Contract Clauses That Help

Prevention is better than enforcement. Include these clauses in your client contracts:

Clear Payment Terms

"Payment is due within 14 days of invoice date. Invoices will be issued upon completion of each milestone/project."

Late Payment Consequences

"Late payments will incur statutory interest under the Late Payment of Commercial Debts (Interest) Act 1998 plus fixed compensation of £70 and all reasonable recovery costs."

Stop Work Clause

"If any payment is overdue by more than 7 days, the Service Provider may suspend all work until payment is received in full."

No-Set-Off Clause

"The Client may not withhold or set-off any payment due against any disputed amounts without prior written agreement."

Delete Your Rights Clause (Avoid!)

Some clients try to include terms that exclude Late Payment Act rights. This is not possible under the Act — any clause that excludes or varies these rights to your detriment is void.

You Cannot Contract Out of the Act

Even if a client's contract says "no interest on late payments" or "payment within 90 days," the Act overrides this. Your statutory rights cannot be removed by contract. However, a more favourable payment term is valid.

Preventing Late Payments

The best enforcement is prevention. Practical steps:

  • Take deposits — 30-50% upfront reduces your exposure
  • Use milestone payments — don't deliver everything before seeing any money
  • Invoice immediately — don't wait until month-end
  • State due dates clearly — remove ambiguity about when payment is expected
  • Include bank details — make payment easy
  • Send reminders before due dates — a quick email nudge
  • Follow up promptly when late — the longer you wait, the harder it gets
  • State Late Payment Act rights — on invoices and in contracts
  • Credit check new clients — know who you're dealing with
  • Have a signed contract — clear terms support your claims

Conclusion

The Late Payment Act 1998 gives UK freelancers and sole traders powerful tools to fight late payment:

  • Statutory interest at Bank of England base rate + 8%
  • Fixed compensation of £40/£70/£100 per invoice
  • Recovery costs for chasing the debt
  • Court enforcement through the Small Claims track

These rights apply automatically — you don't need them in your contract. But stating them clearly educates clients and encourages timely payment.

When payments do go late, follow a systematic approach: reminder, overdue notice, formal demand, Letter Before Action, and finally court proceedings. Be consistent, professional, and persistent.

You did the work. You deserve to be paid. Use your legal rights.
